Passage 1: The Return of the Rooftop Garden

Passage 1 of 3About 20 minutes13 questions

A For most of the twentieth century, the flat roofs of city buildings were treated as wasted space. They held water tanks, air-conditioning units and little else. In the last thirty years, however, architects and planners in many countries have begun to see rooftops as a resource. Gardens planted on roofs, often called green roofs, now cover large areas of cities such as Toronto, Singapore and Copenhagen, and they are increasingly required by law on new commercial buildings.

B The idea itself is not new. Turf roofs were common on farmhouses in Scandinavia for centuries, because a thick layer of soil and grass kept homes warm in winter. What is new is the engineering. A modern green roof is built in layers: a waterproof membrane at the bottom, a barrier to stop roots from damaging it, a drainage layer, a lightweight growing medium and, finally, the plants. Extensive roofs use a shallow layer of soil, sometimes less than ten centimetres, and are planted with hardy species such as sedum that need almost no care. Intensive roofs, by contrast, have deeper soil and can support shrubs, vegetables and even small trees, but they are heavier and more expensive to maintain.

C The most widely cited benefit concerns rainwater. In a conventional city, rain runs off hard surfaces straight into drains. During heavy storms, the drainage system can be overwhelmed, causing floods and, in older cities, the release of untreated sewage into rivers. A green roof acts like a sponge. Studies in temperate climates suggest that such roofs can hold back between half and three-quarters of the rain that falls on them over a year, releasing much of it slowly through evaporation. The effect is smaller during long, intense storms, when the soil quickly becomes saturated, but even a delay of an hour can reduce peak pressure on drains.

D Temperature is a second argument. Dark roofs absorb sunlight and can reach surface temperatures above 70°C on summer afternoons, adding to what is known as the urban heat island effect, in which cities are noticeably warmer than the surrounding countryside. Plants cool their surroundings by shading surfaces and by releasing water vapour. Inside the building, the extra insulation can reduce the energy needed for air conditioning in summer, although the savings in winter heating are usually modest, especially for tall buildings where the roof is only a small part of the total surface.

E Supporters also point to biodiversity. Researchers in Switzerland and the United Kingdom have recorded rare beetles, spiders and ground-nesting birds on roofs that were deliberately planted with local wild species. However, ecologists warn that a roof covered with a single type of sedum offers far less to wildlife than one designed to imitate a natural habitat. A green roof, they argue, is not automatically a nature reserve.

F Cost remains the main obstacle. A green roof can cost two or three times as much to install as a conventional one, and an intensive roof may require a building to be strengthened. Owners are often reluctant to pay because many of the benefits, such as reduced flooding and cooler streets, are enjoyed by the public rather than by the owner. For this reason, several city governments now offer grants or lower drainage charges to buildings with green roofs. Some economists argue that, over the full life of a roof, the costs are partly recovered, since the plant layer protects the waterproof membrane from sunlight and may double its lifespan.

G Not everyone is convinced. Critics note that much of the research has been carried out in cool, wet climates, and that results in hot, dry cities may be very different. A roof that must be watered daily during a desert summer may use more resources than it saves. In such places, designers are experimenting with drought-tolerant native plants and with "blue-green" roofs that store rainwater in tanks beneath the soil. The rooftop garden, it seems, is not a single solution but a family of solutions, each of which must be adapted to its local setting.

Passage 2: How Children Learn to Count

Passage 2 of 3About 20 minutes13 questions

Most adults can count without thinking, and it is easy to assume that counting is a simple skill. Yet research in developmental psychology shows that understanding numbers is a long and surprisingly complex process. A child who can recite "one, two, three, four, five" may still have little idea of what those words mean.

In the 1970s, the psychologists Rochel Gelman and Randy Gallistel proposed a set of principles that a child must grasp in order to count meaningfully. First, each object must be matched with exactly one number word. Second, the number words must always be used in the same order. Third, the last word spoken tells you how many objects there are in the set. This final idea, often called the cardinal principle, turns out to be the most difficult. Ask a three-year-old to count five toy cars and she may do so perfectly; then ask "So how many cars are there?" and she may begin counting again, or give a random number.

Later studies, notably by the psychologist Karen Wynn, showed that children learn the meanings of number words one at a time. At around two and a half, a child may understand that "one" means a single object, while every other number word simply means "more than one". Several months later, the child learns "two", and then "three". Then, quite suddenly, something changes. Children seem to realise that each word in the counting list refers to a set one larger than the word before. From this point, they can give you seven or nine objects as easily as three. Researchers describe these children as "cardinal-principle knowers", and the moment of discovery usually takes place between the ages of three and a half and four.

Why should this step be so hard? One explanation is that number words are unlike most other words a child learns. A word such as "dog" refers to something a child can see and touch. "Five", on the other hand, describes a property of a group, not of any single object in it. Five spoons and five elephants have nothing in common except their number. To learn such a word, the child must ignore almost everything she notices about the objects.

Language itself may play a role. Children who speak languages in which number words follow a very regular pattern appear to learn the structure of larger numbers more quickly. In Chinese, for example, the word for eleven means "ten-one" and the word for twenty-one means "two-ten-one". English learners, by contrast, must memorise irregular forms such as "eleven", "twelve" and "fifteen". Some researchers believe this difference partly explains why children in East Asia often perform well in early arithmetic, although others argue that cultural attitudes and teaching methods are more important.

Studies of communities with very few number words support the view that language matters. Among the Mundurukú people of the Amazon, whose language has exact words only for numbers up to about five, adults can compare large quantities approximately, telling at a glance that a group of eighty dots is larger than one of forty. However, they find it much harder to match exact quantities, for example to set out exactly the same number of objects as in a row of eight. This suggests that humans share an inborn sense of approximate quantity, but that exact counting of large numbers is a cultural tool which depends on words.

These findings have practical consequences. Teachers once assumed that children who could recite numbers to twenty were ready for arithmetic. It now seems that a short, playful test, such as asking a child to "give me four bears", is a far better guide. Parents can also help in everyday ways: by counting stairs, setting the table or sharing fruit, and above all by asking "how many?" after the counting is finished. Such small conversations, research suggests, help children make one of the most important intellectual discoveries of early life.

Passage 3: The Quiet Science of Glacier Archives

Passage 3 of 3About 20 minutes13 questions

A High in mountain ranges and across the polar ice sheets, snow that falls each year is slowly pressed into ice. Over thousands of years, the layers build up like pages in a book. Each layer traps tiny bubbles of air, specks of dust, volcanic ash and chemical traces of the atmosphere at the time it formed. By drilling long cylinders of ice, called ice cores, scientists can read this record and reconstruct the climate of the distant past.

B The deepest cores, drilled in Antarctica, reach more than three kilometres below the surface and contain ice that is around 800,000 years old. Analysis of the air bubbles in these cores has shown that the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere rose and fell in step with temperature through repeated ice ages. During the cold periods it fell to about 180 parts per million, and during the warm periods between them it rose to around 280. Today's level, above 420 parts per million, is far outside this natural range. Scientists are now searching for even older ice, possibly more than a million years old, in places where the ice sheet has moved very little. Such a core could explain why the rhythm of the ice ages appears to have changed in the distant past.

C Temperature itself cannot be measured directly from ice, so researchers rely on an indirect method. Water molecules contain different forms, or isotopes, of oxygen and hydrogen, some slightly heavier than others. When the climate is colder, snow contains a smaller proportion of the heavy isotopes. By measuring this ratio, scientists can estimate the temperature at which the snow formed. The method is not perfect, since changes in the source of moisture can also affect the ratio, but when results are combined with other evidence they give a consistent picture.

D Ice cores also record sudden events. Major volcanic eruptions leave layers rich in sulphate, which can be matched to historical accounts. A sharp sulphate peak in cores from both Greenland and Antarctica, for instance, has been linked to an eruption in the year 1257, later traced to the Samalas volcano in Indonesia. Lead pollution in Greenland ice even reveals the rise and fall of silver mining in the Roman Empire, as smelting released lead into the air that drifted north.

E Mountain glaciers in the tropics and mid-latitudes hold shorter but more local records. Cores from the Andes and the Himalayas show patterns of drought and monsoon rainfall that matter to millions of people living downstream. Unfortunately, these archives are disappearing. Many low-altitude glaciers are melting so quickly that meltwater has already mixed and blurred their upper layers, destroying part of the record before it could be studied.

F In response, an international project has begun to collect cores from threatened glaciers and store them in a purpose-built cave in Antarctica, where the natural temperature stays at around minus 50°C without the need for electricity. The first samples, taken from the Alps, arrived in the early 2020s. Organisers describe the store as a library for scientists of the future, who may have techniques that do not yet exist to analyse the ice.

G Working with ice cores demands great care. Cores are cut into sections, packed in insulated boxes and transported in freezers; a single power failure can ruin years of work. Contamination is another danger, since modern air and even a researcher's breath can alter the chemical signals. Laboratories therefore work at freezing temperatures, and scientists wear protective clothing. Despite these challenges, ice cores remain one of the most valuable sources of information about how Earth's climate system behaves, and how it might respond to further change.
