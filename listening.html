Section 4: A lecture on sleep and memory

Section 4 of 6About 10 minutes10 questions

Show transcript

LECTURER: Today I want to look at the relationship between sleep and memory. For a long time, sleep was seen as a passive state, a kind of rest for the brain. We now know that the brain is extremely active during sleep, and that much of this activity supports learning.

Sleep is divided into stages. The most important for memory appear to be deep sleep, also called slow-wave sleep, and REM sleep, which is when most dreaming occurs. During deep sleep, the brain seems to replay information learned during the day, moving it from a short-term store in an area called the hippocampus to longer-term storage in the cortex.

In one well-known type of experiment, volunteers learn a list of word pairs in the evening. Half then sleep normally, while the other half stay awake. The next morning, those who slept remember on average about twenty per cent more pairs.

REM sleep, by contrast, appears to help with emotional memories and with creative problem-solving, perhaps by connecting ideas in new ways.

What does this mean for students? First, staying up all night before an exam is likely to be counterproductive. Second, even a short nap of around twenty minutes can improve alertness, though longer naps of ninety minutes are needed to include a full sleep cycle. Finally, regular sleep times matter as much as total hours.

Section 4: A talk on the history of the bicycle

Section 6 of 6About 10 minutes10 questions

Show transcript

SPEAKER: The bicycle is so familiar that we rarely think about how it developed. The first two-wheeled machine, built in Germany in 1817, had no pedals at all. Riders pushed it along with their feet, and it was nicknamed the 'running machine'.

Pedals attached to the front wheel arrived in France in the 1860s. Because each turn of the pedals moved the bike only one turn of the wheel, makers increased speed by enlarging the front wheel. The result was the famous 'penny-farthing', which was fast but dangerous, since riders sat high above the ground.

The real breakthrough came in the 1880s with the 'safety bicycle'. It had two wheels of similar size and a chain driving the rear wheel. Shortly afterwards, air-filled tyres made riding far more comfortable.

The social impact was enormous. Bicycles gave ordinary workers cheap transport, and for many women they offered a new sense of independence. Clothing changed too, as long skirts were impractical for cycling.

Today, cities from Amsterdam to Bogotá are investing in cycle lanes, partly to cut pollution and partly to improve public health. Electric bikes, which now make up a third of new bike sales in some European countries, are bringing cycling to older and less fit riders.
