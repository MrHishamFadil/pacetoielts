Reading: The Return of the Night Train

A For most of the late twentieth century, overnight sleeper trains in Europe seemed to belong to the past. Cheap flights could cross the continent in two hours, and many rail companies decided that the cost of running sleeping carriages was no longer worth paying. By 2015, a large number of famous routes had been closed.

B In the last decade, however, night trains have made an unexpected return. One reason is concern about the environment. A passenger travelling by rail between two capital cities produces only a small share of the carbon emissions of the same journey by air. Younger travellers in particular say that this difference influences how they choose to travel.

C Convenience also matters. A traveller who boards a train at ten in the evening and arrives at eight the next morning saves the price of a hotel night. In addition, railway stations are usually in city centres, while airports are often an hour's drive away. Business travellers who need to attend morning meetings have found this especially useful.

D Nevertheless, the revival faces real difficulties. Sleeping carriages are expensive to build, and each one holds far fewer passengers than an ordinary seated carriage. As a result, tickets can cost more than a flight on the same route. Operators also complain that track charges, the fees paid to use the rails in each country, differ widely and make planning across borders complicated.

E Some governments have responded with support. A few have reduced track charges for night services, and others have helped to pay for new carriages. Supporters argue that this is fair, since airlines have long benefited from tax rules on fuel. Critics reply that public money should go first to everyday commuter trains, which carry far more people.

F Whether night trains will become a normal part of travel again is still uncertain. What seems clear is that many passengers are willing to give them a second chance, provided the journey is comfortable and the price is reasonable.
